# Referee report

Page references below refer to the printed pages of the manuscript. DHTS-2026-10-12.pdf

## 1. Summary

The paper develops a system for segmenting census images and recognizing handwritten names, trains it on 1940 census records for which Ancestry and FamilySearch transcriptions agree, and substitutes machine transcriptions for Ancestry names on records where the two human sources disagree. It evaluates the consequences for linking 1930–1940 census records and linking the 1940 census to Social Security mortality records. The principal reported result is a 119 percent increase in linkage among targeted records under the main exact-name procedure, alongside an approximately 8 percent increase overall. Middle-initial comparisons and genealogical reference links are used to assess link quality, and additional exercises use Jaro–Winkler matching and HLINK. The paper argues that improving transcription inputs recovers otherwise missed links, expands coverage of underlinked populations, and is especially useful in enumeration districts with low transcription agreement.

## 2. Major concerns

### 2.1. The missing FamilySearch baseline prevents attribution of the gains to machine learning

**Sections 2.3 and 3.2, pp. 9 and 12; Tables 4–9.** The relevant comparison is not exhausted by Ancestry versus the proposed replacement policy. The authors already possess another transcription of every targeted record. On the all-age, both-sex disagreement sample described on p. 9, the machine output agrees with FamilySearch in 52 percent of cases and with Ancestry in 14 percent. This does not establish which transcription is correct, but it makes the missing comparison indispensable: **how much improvement would arise simply from replacing Ancestry with FamilySearch?**

Run the same linkage procedures on a fixed population under at least three policies: Ancestry names, FamilySearch replacement on the target set, and machine replacement on that same set. A fourth useful benchmark would allow both human spellings under a prespecified ambiguity-resolution rule, evaluated for quality as well as coverage. An unrestricted union of links would not be an adequate benchmark.

The results should also distinguish machine outputs that reproduce FamilySearch, reproduce Ancestry, or match neither. Without these comparisons, the paper establishes the value of changing transcription inputs, but not the incremental value of its machine-learning procedure over information already available to the researcher.

### 2.2. The replacement policy is underspecified at precisely the points that can change linkage outcomes

**Section 2.3, p. 9; Section 3.2, p. 12; Appendix A.** Ten percent of discordant records receive “no usable transcription.” What happens to those records in the linkage files? Do they retain Ancestry names, receive FamilySearch names, lose one name field, or disappear from the candidate pool? Likewise, does disagreement in one field trigger replacement of both fields, or only the disputed field?

These are substantive choices. Removing a difficult record from the candidate pool can make another record’s previously ambiguous match unique. Such a gain need not reflect successful recognition of the difficult name.

The p. 9 reference to a “ditto-resolution rule our pipeline has in common with FamilySearch” also needs an operational description. The appendix explains how ditto-prone observations are excluded from training, but not how ditto marks are resolved during deployment.

Provide a deterministic replacement-and-fallback rule, with counts for every outcome in the actual linkage samples. Separate recognition, normalization, ditto resolution, and fallback to existing transcriptions. The PDF does not permit assessment of how these components contribute to the reported gains.

### 2.3. The headline 28 percent improvement in “match quality” changes the validation measurement

**Abstract; introduction, p. 2; Section 4.1 and Table 4, p. 15.** The arithmetic behind 65.4 to 83.6 percent middle-initial agreement is correct. Its interpretation as a 28 percent improvement in match quality is not supported by the paper’s own decomposition. Page 15 states that only **8–10 of the approximately 18 percentage-point increase remains when both link sets are evaluated against the same 1940 middle initial**. The remainder reflects changing the transcription of the validation variable.

That qualification materially changes the headline. It should appear wherever the 28 percent figure appears, or the headline should use the fixed-initial result instead.

The fixed-initial exercise needs a table, not only prose. Report old and new link sets scored using a fixed Ancestry initial and, separately, a fixed FamilySearch initial, with sample sizes and results for retained, added, and removed pairs. Freeze the source-record rules determining initial availability and report missingness separately. The stated 82–85 versus 71 percent comparison also needs denominators.

Apply the same treatment to Tables 5–9. Their notes do not establish whether the validating 1940 initial remains fixed. Finally, middle-initial agreement is an informative proxy, not match precision: correct pairs can disagree, incorrect pairs can agree, and only a selected subset has initials in both sources.

### 2.4. The genealogical reference standard is insufficiently documented to establish independence

**Section 4.2, pp. 16–17; Tables 5–8.** “A set of high-quality genealogical links built from FamilySearch profiles” is not enough information to assess the central validation exercise. The paper needs to identify the dataset or extraction date, construction procedure, inclusion criteria, and supporting evidence. In particular:

- Are reference links manually attached census records, algorithmically generated links, or a mixture?
- What evidence beyond the two census transcriptions supports the attachments?
- How are duplicate profiles, multiple attachments, and conflicting partners resolved?
- Do reference pairs or profiles overlap HLINK training or tuning data?
- Was the reference set used to select transcription rules or model versions?

Using FamilySearch in both consensus training labels and genealogical validation does not, by itself, prove leakage. It does mean that independent errors cannot be assumed merely because the validation file is external to the census extract.

Document provenance and overlap, freeze the reference set, and evaluate on held-out profiles or families where necessary. Report reference coverage by the demographic and legibility groups central to the paper. Otherwise, “verified” remains agreement with an inadequately characterized reference rather than demonstrated independent confirmation.

### 2.5. A falling contradicted share does not establish that overall precision improves

**Section 4.2, especially p. 17.** The paper correctly acknowledges that directly contradicted links provide a lower bound on errors. It then draws a stronger conclusion: the increases in contradicted counts do not represent an accuracy cost because their share falls.

That inference does not follow when a large, changing fraction of links is unverifiable. Let \(V\) be verified links, \(C\) contradicted links, and \(L\) all links. Even treating the reference as error-free, the evidence establishes only

\[
\frac{V}{L}\leq \text{precision}\leq 1-\frac{C}{L}.
\]

An increase in the upper bound does not establish an increase in precision. In Table 7, the verified fraction actually declines from approximately **42.46 to 42.15 percent**, while the contradicted fraction also declines; the unverifiable fraction increases.

The defensible finding is that verified counts rise and observed contradiction shares fall. The sentence that the gains are “not bought with spurious matches” goes further. Show results within a fixed reference-covered population, report changing reference coverage, and independently audit a sample of added and unverifiable pairs. In Panel B, “unverifiable” should be described as **unknown**, not “neutral.”

### 2.6. Targeted gains conceal substantial losses outside the target set

**Section 4.1, pp. 15–16; Tables 5–8.** The explanation that aggregate effects are smaller because approximately 72 percent of records received no transcription changes suggests dilution by an unaffected group. The tables imply a different accounting.

Summing Panel A’s three validation categories and subtracting the target-sample changes from the full-sample changes gives:

| Linker | Net change in all links | Net change in targeted links | Implied change outside target | Implied change in verified links outside target |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| ABE, Tables 5–6 | +972,032 | +1,520,423 | **−548,391** | **−282,223** |
| Jaro–Winkler, Tables 5–6 | +807,627 | +1,080,557 | **−272,930** | **−146,035** |
| HLINK, Tables 7–8 | +1,689,889 | +2,105,715 | **−415,826** | **−181,547** |

These are accounting implications if the target samples are subsets “restricted throughout,” as the notes state. Table 4’s rounded rates likewise imply approximately **0.60 million fewer links among congruent records**.

Changing names can alter candidate competition and uniqueness even for records whose names remain unchanged. The relevant estimand is therefore the net effect of the replacement policy on the entire linkage system. Report transitions—retained pairs, added pairs, removed pairs, and changed partners—separately for targeted, congruent, and unavailable-comparison records. Explain how many lost links were genuinely erroneous and how many were verified links displaced by the new candidate structure. The mechanism cannot be determined from the PDF alone.

### 2.7. The robustness exercises need a common analysis population and a fully specified implementation

**Sections 3.1–3.3 and 4.2; Tables 4–8.** The paper discloses that Table 4 uses men aged 10 and above with complete comparison fields, Tables 5–6 use men aged 8 and above, and Tables 7–8 include both sexes aged 8 and above. That disclosure prevents a hidden denominator error, but does not supply the comparison needed to assess how the benefit changes across linkage methods.

Provide results for all linkers on a common male, age-10-plus analysis population, with the candidate universes and replacement policy documented. Present women and ages 8–9 as additional samples. Explain why ages 8–9 enter an exercise linking to a census ten years earlier, given the explicit exclusion rationale on p. 11; an allowance for age misreporting should be stated and evaluated.

The phrase “algorithm held fixed” also needs operational content: software version, blocking, name cleaning, matching variables, thresholds, and whether HLINK weights were reused or retrained. Mentioning separate codebases on pp. 11–12 is not a substitute for this specification.

For Table 9, state the 1940-side eligibility rules explicitly. Its clear BUNMD denominator does not tell the reader whether the census side includes all men or retains the earlier age restriction.

### 2.8. The proposed name validation will not resolve the transcription-accuracy question as designed

**Section 4.4, pp. 22–23; Table 1; Appendix A.4.** Requiring at least one of the three transcriptions to match the genealogical profile name selects the evaluation sample using the outcomes being evaluated. It excludes cases where every method fails and can change both accuracy levels and relative rankings. It does not simply remove legitimate name changes.

There are two further problems. A genealogical profile’s preferred name need not equal what the enumerator wrote, and the presence of birth-date information does not establish that independently documented non-census evidence supports the profile. Literal transcription accuracy and agreement with a person’s preferred or legal name are different targets.

For transcription accuracy, draw a prespecified sample of images, including disagreement cases, agreement cases, and machine failures, and obtain blinded adjudications with sufficient page and household context. Report unresolved handwriting separately. For identity accuracy, document the independent sources supporting the reference identity and do not condition inclusion on any evaluated transcription succeeding.

The existing test split also needs clarification. A 10 percent record holdout may share pages, enumerators, households, or alternative crops with training. Coordinate splits across first-name training, surname transfer learning, and all image variants; report a page/enumerator or geographic holdout. I cannot establish leakage from the PDF, but the current description does not establish performance on unfamiliar handwriting.

### 2.9. The architecture does not establish the claimed absence of common-name bias

**Introduction, pp. 2–4; Section 2.2, pp. 7–8; Appendix A.3.** Independent output heads do not imply independence from name-frequency priors. The heads share an EfficientNet representation of the image and are trained on a highly unequal distribution of names and character patterns. Page 7 itself says the model learns patterns such as the frequency of “John” and “Mary.”

The narrower architectural claim is that predictions are not constrained to an explicit whole-name dictionary. The repeated claim that the approach “avoids” common-name bias requires evidence.

Report accuracy and normalization toward common alternatives by name frequency, including names absent from training. Compare at least one credible alternative handwriting recognizer on the same crops and training labels. Li et al.’s **TrOCR** is one concrete comparator; its existence also makes comparisons solely with generic printed-text OCR inadequate. The paper need not become a comprehensive recognition benchmark, but “higher accuracy” and the claimed architectural advantage require an actual alternative. [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.48550/ARXIV.2109.10282?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 2.10. The legibility result combines treatment prevalence with effectiveness

**Section 4.5, p. 25; Figure 4.** Legibility is defined by agreement between the human transcriptions, while replacement is targeted at disagreement. Low-legibility districts therefore receive more interventions by construction: the reported target shares are **46.2 versus 14.0 percent** in the lowest and highest quartiles. Their baseline linkage rates also differ.

Consequently, a 20 versus 1.6 percent district-wide gain does not by itself establish that the model is more effective at reading difficult handwriting. It combines treatment prevalence, baseline linkage, recovery within the target set, and effects on other records.

Decompose these components and report quality among targeted records within each legibility group. The aggregate benefit in low-legibility districts is a useful operational result; the stronger recognition-mechanism claim requires additional evidence.

## 3. Internal consistency

### 3.1. Panels A and B of Tables 5–8 fail exact accounting identities

This is the clearest numerical inconsistency. With a fixed reference standard and pair-level categories, each category \(k\) must satisfy

\[
N_{\mathrm{new},k}-N_{\mathrm{old},k}
=
N_{\mathrm{added},k}-N_{\mathrm{removed},k}.
\]

It does not. For example, Table 7’s verified count rises by

\[
28{,}863{,}711-28{,}357{,}041=506{,}670,
\]

but its added-minus-removed verified count is

\[
1{,}135{,}269-633{,}350=501{,}919.
\]

Its contradicted count rises by **2,347** in Panel A but by **10,384** in Panel B.

The discrepancies occur throughout:

| Specification | Verified residual | Unverifiable residual | Contradicted residual |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Table 5: Jaro–Winkler | +2,601 | +1,134 | −3,735 |
| Table 5: ABE | +1,189 | +707 | −1,896 |
| Table 6: Jaro–Winkler | +2,993 | +1,094 | −4,087 |
| Table 6: ABE | +1,630 | +687 | −2,317 |
| Table 7: HLINK | +4,751 | +3,286 | −8,037 |
| Table 8: HLINK | +2,333 | +1,159 | −3,492 |

Here “residual” is Panel A’s change minus Panel B’s added-minus-removed count. Overall link totals reconcile; classifications do not. An unchanged pair cannot change category against the same fixed reference under the stated rules.

Regenerate both panels from one pair-level classification and explain any intentional differences in definitions. These are not rounding discrepancies. The PDF does not reveal their coding cause, and the central validation claims should not be finalized until they are resolved.

### 3.2. The main and robustness middle-initial results require reconciliation

**Tables 4 and 6.** Baseline target-sample middle-initial agreement is **65.4 percent** in Table 4 but **76.81 percent** under exact-name ABE in Table 6. Yet the baseline linked counts are nearly identical: approximately 1.283 million implied by Table 4 versus 1,284,239 in Table 6.

Different universes and implementations may explain this; it is not a demonstrated arithmetic error. But an 11.4 percentage-point quality difference is too large to leave under the general statement that the robustness exercise reproduces the main result. Reconcile sample selection, initial extraction, initial source, and linkage rules explicitly.

### 3.3. Definitions and denominators remain incomplete

**Sections 3.3–4.3; Tables 4–9.**

- **Recall:** Define it algebraically and print the exact number of eligible reference pairs. The denominator is not the displayed population denominator. The values imply reference populations of roughly 19.47 million in Table 5, 4.63 million in Table 6, 36.13 million in Table 7, and 8.80 million in Table 8, but readers should not have to reconstruct these.
- **Validated versus verified:** Reserve distinct terms for middle-initial agreement and genealogical-reference agreement. Neither is synonymous with adjudicated truth.
- **Table 4’s “# of records”:** Clarify that the column changes meaning between the linkage universe and the initial-available subset, and supply actual linked counts.
- **Tables 5–8:** Supply initial-available sample sizes and their shares of all links. Table 9 already demonstrates why this matters: validation coverage falls from 25.32 to 24.61 percent for exact matches even while agreement rises.
- **Token accuracy, Table 1 and Appendix A:** Specify whether spaces and padding positions enter the denominator, how length and insertion/deletion errors are scored, and whether accuracy is pooled over characters or averaged over names.

Most other displayed arithmetic checks out: the match rates in Tables 5–8 reproduce from their counts and denominators, the contradiction shares quoted on pp. 16–17 reproduce, and Table 9’s count changes and relative gains reproduce. Table 2’s analysis means are compatible with Table 3 after rounding. Apparent discrepancies such as Table 4’s 119 percent headline versus its rounded rates are not, by themselves, errors.

### 3.4. The matching definitions do not imply the properties asserted

**Section 3.2, p. 11.** Requiring each 1930 record to have exactly one 1940 candidate does not ensure one-to-one linkage: two 1930 records can share the same sole candidate. Explain reciprocal uniqueness or the conflict-resolution procedure that produces the one-to-one files asserted in Section 4.2.

**Section 4.3 and Table 9, pp. 21–22.** Exact matches are already described as unique within the ±2-year window in both files. The conservative variant then excludes records with competing matches within that same window. As written, the additional restriction appears redundant, although the resulting counts differ substantially. Specify whether ordinary matching uses age-priority searches, whether conservative uniqueness is assessed within each source, or whether “competing” refers to a broader candidate set.

Also replace “birth state matches exactly” in the formal description with the appropriate harmonized birthplace rule for foreign-born people.

## 4. Framing and positioning

### 4.1. The headlines should emphasize aggregate usable gains and identify the linkage method

The 119 percent result is relevant, but it is a gain for a selected difficult-record subset under a particular matching procedure. Users deciding whether to adopt the transcriptions also need the full-sample gains and the performance with a stronger linker.

Calculated from the counts in Tables 5–8:

| Linker | Increase in all links | Increase in targeted links |
|---|---:|---:|
| ABE | 7.77% | 118.39% |
| Jaro–Winkler | 6.37% | 48.44% |
| HLINK | 2.53% | 13.78% |

These are within-specification changes; the differing populations still prevent treating them as clean comparisons across algorithms. The abstract should report an aggregate result alongside the targeted doubling, and replace the unqualified 28 percent “match quality” claim with an appropriately defined validation result.

### 4.2. Two particularly close predecessors by members of the author team are missing

**Introduction, pp. 3–4; references, pp. 28–30.** The claim that decomposition into table identification, cell extraction, and recognition is the “key methodological innovation” needs to distinguish this paper from:

- **Dahl, Johansen, Sørensen, and Wittrock (2023), “HANA: A handwritten name database for offline handwritten text recognition,” *Explorations in Economic History* 87, 101473.** This already provides handwritten-name training data and demonstrates transfer learning for both Danish and U.S. census transcription. Explain what is new in the training resources, model, application, and downstream evaluation. [University of Southern Denmark](https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/en/publications/hana-a-handwritten-name-database-for-offline-handwritten-text-rec-2/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
- **Dahl, Johansen, Sørensen, Westermann, and Wittrock (2023), “Applications of machine learning in tabular document digitisation,” *Historical Methods* 56(1), 34–48.** This already develops the layout-classification, segmentation, and handwriting-recognition pipeline. Identify inherited components and the present additions. [University of Bristol](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/applications-of-machine-learning-in-tabular-document-digitisation/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
- **Petitpierre, Kramer, and Rappo (2023), “An end-to-end pipeline for historical censuses processing,” *IJDAR* 26, 419–432.** This directly addresses census layout extraction, handwriting recognition, and post-correction, and is relevant to the claims about dense historical tables and international generalization. [Springer Nature Link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10032-023-00428-9?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The strongest distinctive contribution appears to be the national-scale implementation and evaluation of transcription replacement across several linkage tasks. Whether particular technical components are also new requires a more explicit comparison with these predecessors.

The p. 6 characterization of LayoutParser as designed for newspaper articles should also be corrected: it is a general document-layout toolkit with multiple pretrained models and support for custom training. An unsuitable newspaper-trained model is not a limitation of the entire toolkit. [layout-parser.github.io](https://layout-parser.github.io/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 4.3. Coverage improvements should not be presented as demonstrated improvements in representativeness

**Abstract; Section 4.5; conclusion.** Page 25 acknowledges that proportional gains are smaller for non-white than white residents: **6.4 versus 7.9 percent**. Applied to the reported baselines, the absolute racial linkage-rate gap grows from 13.1 to approximately **14.3 percentage points**. The no-schooling group also gains less proportionally than the overall sample.

The claim that more records become available for these groups is supported. A claim that demographic linkage disparities narrow is not. The conclusion’s qualification about not establishing reduced estimation bias should govern the introduction as well. Likewise, Table 2 supports similarity on its displayed observables, not the broader p. 12 assertion that excluding approximately 4.31 million records does not select a materially different population.

### 4.4. Scalability needs measured costs and a concrete dissemination plan

**Sections 2.2–2.3, pp. 8–10; conclusion.** The only compute benchmark is 1,600 GPU-hours for 299,880 records, extrapolated to approximately 730,000 GPU-hours nationally. Since the national run is reported as completed, provide its actual resource use: GPU model, training versus inference, segmentation cost, throughput, and relevant human setup effort. Tuning and larger batches make a lower cost plausible; they do not establish the claimed upper bound from the PDF.

For this audience, cost per additional verified link—especially relative to the FamilySearch baseline—is more useful than an unspecified GPU-hour total. State what researchers will receive: replacement names keyed to census identifiers, confidence scores, failure flags, code, model weights, and any access restrictions.

The claimed portability to singly transcribed or untranscribed archives should also be qualified. The demonstrated training and targeting procedure depends on two existing transcriptions and census-specific templates; transfer to other settings remains to be shown.

## 5. Structure and exposition

1. **Compress repeated methodological claims.** The introduction’s p. 4 paragraph repeats the measurement-versus-linker contribution. Section 2.2 repeatedly explains ambiguous characters and confidence scores, while Section 2.3 repeats the easy-test-set qualification. Appendix A.3–A.4 repeats metric definitions and results. Use that space for the replacement policy, test splits, and missing implementation details.

2. **Organize validation before making quality claims.** Introduce the reference set and validation definitions in the data/methods section. Then present coverage, validation, and transition accounting together. Tables 5–8 currently serve both robustness and validation functions without a sufficiently developed description of the validation data.

3. **Move or complete Section 4.4.** An unfinished validation proposal interrupts the results between mortality linkage and heterogeneity. Complete an appropriately redesigned exercise, or move the proposal to limitations/future work. Update the p. 15 roadmap, which promises heterogeneity before final validation but does not describe the actual ordering.

4. **Replace operational narrative with research-relevant specifications.** The extended discussion of empty Hawaiian extracts, Alaska’s absence from script lists, and separate coauthor codebases can be shortened. Retain exact coverage and reproducibility requirements; move operational details to a data appendix. Conversely, the county paragraph on p. 25 needs a supporting table or figure, weighting rules, and a defined sample. Its reported percentiles and compositional associations currently cannot be assessed.

## 7. Overall assessment

The strongest contribution is the evaluation of transcription as an adjustable input to historical linkage, including the ability to measure consequences across multiple linkage procedures and a mortality application. That is directly useful to economists constructing historical panels.

The biggest journal risk is that the paper currently attributes too much to the machine-learning method without the readily available FamilySearch comparison, while its strongest quality claims depend on changing validation measurements and incompletely documented reference evidence. The category-accounting failures in Tables 5–8 compound that risk. I would require substantial revision before accepting the headline claims.

The three changes with the greatest payoff are:

1. **Establish incremental value:** add FamilySearch and a carefully controlled dual-transcription benchmark on common samples, with an explicit replacement/failure policy.
2. **Make quality evidence auditable:** reconcile Tables 5–8, document and separate the genealogical reference, display fixed-initial results, and conduct blinded adjudication that includes difficult records and failures.
3. **Report the complete net effect:** account for gains and losses inside and outside the target set, then revise the headline numbers and contribution claims around those results and the closest prior literature.
