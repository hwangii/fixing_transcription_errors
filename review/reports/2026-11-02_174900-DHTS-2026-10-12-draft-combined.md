## How this report was built

- **Verified** = recomputed from the table files or confirmed in the .tex source.
- **Plausible** = cannot be checked from the PDF alone (depends on code, data, or a figure).
- **Check** = a factual claim about an outside source that should be confirmed before acting.

Section 7 lists claims from the source reports that were checked and found to be wrong; they are excluded from the body. Line numbers refer to DHTS-2026-10-12.tex; page numbers to the PDF compiled 2026-11-01.

## 1. Summary

The paper trains a segmentation-plus-recognition pipeline (FilterReg point-set registration; EfficientNetV2-S with 40 position-specific character heads) on 1940 census records where the Ancestry and FamilySearch transcriptions agree, then substitutes its transcriptions for Ancestry's on the 28.2% of records where the two human sources disagree. It evaluates the effect on 1930–1940 linkage under exact-name ABE, Jaro-Winkler ABE and HLINK, and on 1940-to-BUNMD mortality linkage. Headline results: the ABE linkage rate on discordant records rises from 8.9% to 19.4% (+119%), the overall rate from 22.8% to 24.6% (+8%), and target-set middle-initial agreement from 65.4% to 83.6% (+28%). Verified-correct links against a FamilySearch genealogical reference rise in every specification while the share of links the reference contradicts falls; gains are largest in low-legibility enumeration districts.

All three reviewers agree the core contribution is real and useful: treating transcription as an improvable input rather than a fixed one, demonstrated at full-census scale across several linkers. All three also agree the paper, as written, attributes more to the machine-learning method than its evidence supports, and that several tables need reconciliation before the quality claims can be finalized.

## 2. Major concerns

Ordered by how much they bear on the main claims. The first three were raised independently by all three reviewers.

### 2.1 Attribution: the missing FamilySearch baseline [GPT, Gemini, Claude — verified]

**Where:** Sections 2.3, 3.2, 4.1–4.2; Tables 4–9.

On discordant records the model reproduces FamilySearch's string in 52% of cases and Ancestry's in 14% (p. 9). The "before" links use Ancestry only. Without a column that substitutes the FamilySearch transcription on the same target set, the paper cannot separate "machine learning reads handwriting better" from "Ancestry's reading of hard records is worse than FamilySearch's, and the model reproduces FamilySearch."

FamilySearch's census transcription is not available to researchers, so for a user of IPUMS the Ancestry-vs-ML comparison is the practically relevant one. That does not remove the referee point: the authors hold the FamilySearch strings on every target record (they define the target set and the training labels), so they are the only party who can run the comparison, and a referee seeing 52% agreement and no FamilySearch column will assume the worst. Run internally and reported, the result likely strengthens the paper: "FamilySearch's transcription is proprietary; ours is public, recovers most of what it would yield, and adds links neither human source could."

**Resolve by:** (a) a FamilySearch-substitution column on the target set in Tables 4–9, and ideally a prespecified either-name rule; (b) linkage and validation outcomes decomposed across the four cells on p. 9 (14% agrees with Ancestry, 52% with FamilySearch, 24% neither, 10% no output) — the model's independent contribution lives in the 24% cell; (c) a paragraph stating that targeting requires a second transcription, and what a researcher with only IPUMS should do with the distributed names.

### 2.2 Net accounting: links lost among congruent records [GPT, Gemini, Claude — verified]

**Where:** Section 4.1 (p. 16); Tables 4–8.

The text says aggregate gains are smaller because 72% of records "received no transcription improvements," implying dilution by an untouched group. The tables imply instead that those records *lose* links. Subtracting the restricted tables from the full ones:

| Linker | Net change, all links | Net change, target links | Implied change outside target | Implied change in verified links outside target |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| ABE (T5 − T6) | +972,032 | +1,520,423 | −548,391 | −282,223 |
| Jaro-Winkler (T5 − T6) | +807,627 | +1,080,557 | −272,930 | −146,035 |
| HLINK (T7 − T8) | +1,689,889 | +2,105,715 | −415,826 | −181,547 |

Table 4's rounded rates imply roughly 0.6 million fewer links among congruent records. Even within the target set, Panel B shows 147,928 verified-correct ABE links removed (226,946 under HLINK). The likely mechanism is uniqueness collisions — a re-transcribed discordant record now duplicates a congruent record within the same birth state and ±2-year window, so ABE drops both — but it is also possible the Section 4.2 codebase replaced names on all records rather than only discordant ones. The PDF cannot distinguish these.

**Resolve by:** state which records had names replaced in each linkage run; report transitions (retained, added, removed, partner-switched) separately for target and congruent records; consider a rule that uses model confidence to replace only when the model is confident, so that re-transcription does not destroy half a million existing links. Rewrite the p. 16 sentence.

### 2.3 The 28% "match quality" headline changes the yardstick [GPT, Gemini, Claude — verified]

**Where:** Abstract; p. 2; Section 4.1 and Table 4.

The before column scores links against Ancestry's 1940 middle initial; the after column scores against the model's own re-reading of it. The text concedes (p. 15) that only 8–10 of the 18 percentage points survive when both are scored against the same initial. The unconfounded figure from Table 6 (ABE, discordant, consistent initial field) is 76.81% → 83.51%, about +8.7%, not +28%. The fixed-initial decomposition and the 82–85% vs 71% comparison (lines 549–551) exist only in prose with no denominators.

**Resolve by:** a table reporting old and new link sets scored against a fixed Ancestry initial and separately a fixed FamilySearch initial, for retained, added and removed pairs; state whether the initial is held fixed in Tables 5–9; use the fixed-initial number in the abstract and introduction. Middle-initial agreement is a proxy, not precision; say so.

### 2.4 Quality of the added links, judged by the reference [Gemini — verified; GPT partially]

**Where:** Section 4.2 (p. 17); Tables 5–8 Panel B.

The paper's "contradicted share falls" uses all links as denominator, which dilutes with unverifiable links (70% of HLINK additions). Among links the reference can actually judge, added links are contradicted far more often than the existing stock:

| Specification | Contradicted / (verified + contradicted), old links | Same, added links |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Table 5, Jaro-Winkler | 4.7% | 8.3% |
| Table 5, ABE | 5.3% | 7.0% |
| Table 7, HLINK | 1.7% | 8.0% |
| Table 8, HLINK discordant | 1.9% | 6.1% |

The defensible statement is that added links are still 92–94% verified-correct when judgeable, but are of lower quality than the baseline stock. GPT's bound makes the same point formally: V/L ≤ precision ≤ 1 − C/L, and a rising upper bound does not establish rising precision (in Table 7 the verified share actually falls, 42.46% → 42.15%). The sentence "the gains are not bought with spurious matches" (line 580) overreaches.

**Resolve by:** add a contradicted-among-verifiable row to Panel A and Panel B; show results within a fixed reference-covered population; relabel "Neutral" as "Unknown"; soften line 580.

### 2.5 Independence of the genealogical reference standard [GPT, Gemini, Claude — plausible]

**Where:** p. 3; Section 4.2 (pp. 16–17).

The reference is "built from FamilySearch profiles." FamilySearch attaches census records to profiles largely through hints generated from its own index, i.e. its own transcription. If the model agrees with FamilySearch four times as often as with Ancestry, links built on the model's names will mechanically score higher against a FamilySearch-indexed truth set, independent of which reading is correct. The paper calls this "evidence from outside the census" (line 573); it is not evidence from outside FamilySearch's transcription.

**Resolve by:** document the reference set (source, extraction date, manual vs. algorithmic attachment, duplicate and conflict handling, overlap with HLINK training data, whether it informed any model choice); test whether the verified-correct gain survives on records where the model disagrees with FamilySearch; report reference coverage by demographic and legibility group.

### 2.6 Out-of-sample generalization and the test split [Claude, GPT — plausible]

**Where:** Sections 2.1, 2.3; Appendix A.4.

The model was trained on Rhode Island plus "selected regions of other states" (line 184) and applied to 49 jurisdictions, but accuracy is reported only on a 10% held-out slice of the same pool, which may share pages, enumerators and households with training. Nothing reports performance on a state the model never saw, and Section 2 does not say whether the model was retrained after the expansion.

**Resolve by:** Table 1 and the 66/14/52/24/10 breakdown split by in-training vs. out-of-training states; a page- or enumerator-level holdout; a sentence on whether and when the model was retrained.

### 2.7 Replacement policy and the 10% with no transcription [GPT, Gemini, Claude — verified]

**Where:** Section 2.3 (p. 9); Section 3.2.

For 3.4 million discordant records the pipeline returns nothing. The text never says what name enters the linker for them (Ancestry? FamilySearch? dropped?), whether disagreement in one field triggers replacement of both, or how ditto marks are resolved at inference (the appendix covers only their exclusion from training). Removing a hard record from the candidate pool can make a neighbour's match unique, which is a gain that has nothing to do with reading the name.

**Resolve by:** a deterministic replacement-and-fallback rule with counts for each outcome in the actual linkage samples.

### 2.8 Legibility result conflates prevalence with effectiveness [GPT — verified from text]

**Where:** Section 4.5 (p. 25); Figure 4.

Legibility is defined by human agreement and replacement is triggered by disagreement, so low-legibility districts receive more interventions by construction (46.2% vs 14.0% of records). A larger district-wide gain (20% vs 1.6%) therefore does not show the model reads hard handwriting better; it combines treatment prevalence, baseline rate and within-target recovery.

**Resolve by:** report linkage and quality gains among targeted records within each legibility quartile; keep the district-level result as an operational finding.

### 2.9 Figure 3 and the under-linked-groups framing [Gemini — plausible; GPT, Claude on the framing]

**Where:** Abstract; p. 2 (line 92); Section 4.5 (lines 718–744).

The text tells readers to look for groups "to the left and high up"; the numbers quoted in the text say the opposite. Non-white men have the lowest baseline (11.0%) and the lowest proportional gain (6.4%); no schooling 11.9% / 7.0%; inmates 15.0% / 7.3%; whites 24.1% / 7.9%. The racial gap widens from 13.1 to roughly 14.3 percentage points. Line 92's claim that training data were built "with particular attention to names from minority and immigrant populations" is unsupported: consensus-only training underrepresents exactly those names (Table 3). Table 3 also shows Black men have a *lower* disagreement rate, so their low baseline is not mainly a handwriting problem.

**Resolve by:** remove the line 92 claim; describe Figure 3 accurately (gains positively correlated with baseline); say coverage rises for every group but disparities do not narrow; let the conclusion's caution govern the introduction.

### 2.10 "Avoids common-name bias" is asserted, not shown [GPT — verified from text]

**Where:** pp. 2–4, Section 2.2; Appendix A.3.

Independent output heads share one EfficientNet representation trained on a highly skewed name distribution; line 236 says the model learns "the frequency of John or Mary." The defensible claim is "no explicit whole-name dictionary," not "avoids bias."

**Resolve by:** report accuracy and drift toward common alternatives by name frequency, including names absent from training; compare against one credible HTR baseline (e.g. TrOCR) on the same crops.

### 2.11 Section 4.4 should be completed or cut [GPT, Gemini, Claude]

Two pages describe an unrun exercise ending "results are forthcoming." Its criterion (3) — keep only records where at least one transcription matches the profile exactly — selects on the outcome and drops every hard case. Simplest fix for this submission: cut to two sentences in the conclusion. If run, drop criterion (3) and use edit distance against profiles anchored in non-census vital records.

## 3. Internal consistency

### 3.1 Panels A and B of Tables 5–8 fail the accounting identity [GPT, Gemini — verified]

With pair-level categories against a fixed reference, New − Old must equal Added − Removed within each category. Totals reconcile exactly in every column; categories do not:

| Specification | Verified residual | Unverifiable residual | Contradicted residual |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Table 5, Jaro-Winkler | +2,601 | +1,134 | −3,735 |
| Table 5, ABE | +1,189 | +707 | −1,896 |
| Table 6, Jaro-Winkler | +2,993 | +1,094 | −4,087 |
| Table 6, ABE | +1,630 | +687 | −2,317 |
| Table 7, HLINK | +4,751 | +3,286 | −8,037 |
| Table 8, HLINK | +2,333 | +1,159 | −3,492 |

Residual = (New − Old) − (Added − Removed). They sum to zero in each column, so a few thousand *retained* pairs move from contradicted to verified or unverifiable between runs. That cannot happen under the stated rules unless classification depends on something beyond the pair (e.g. contradiction assessed against other links in the same file) or Panel B is counted at the person level so partner switches are invisible. This lives in the LINKAGE code on machine B and should be resolved before Section 4.2 is revised.

### 3.2 Baseline middle-initial rates disagree across tables [GPT, Gemini — verified]

Table 4 reports 65.4% baseline MI agreement on the target set; Table 6 reports 76.81% under exact-name ABE. The underlying link sets are nearly identical (≈1.283M vs 1,284,239). Different initial extraction or sample rules may explain an 11.4-point gap, but the text must say what does.

### 3.3 Four universes, three age cutoffs [GPT, Gemini, Claude — verified]

- Table 4: men 10+, all four name fields and age present, 51,036,859.
- Tables 5–6: men 8+, 57,589,273, no name-field restriction.
- Tables 7–8: men and women 8+, 115,030,746.
- Section 2.3: both sexes, all ages, 35.1M discordant.
- Table 9: BUNMD men born ≤ 1940; the 1940-side eligibility rule is not stated.

Children aged 8–9 in April 1940 were born after April 1930 and cannot have a true 1930 match, so every link for them is a false positive admitted by the ±2 window; the text's own rationale for the age-10 cutoff (line 400) applies. The robustness linkages should be rerun on the paper's own sample definition, or the paper should say why they cannot be.

### 3.4 Definitions missing or inconsistent [GPT, Gemini, Claude]

- **Recall** is never defined; the implied reference-pair denominators (≈19.5M, 4.6M, 36.1M, 8.8M) should be printed.
- **Validated** (middle initials) and **verified** (genealogical reference) are used near-interchangeably; reserve distinct terms.
- **Table 4 "# of records"** means the denominator in the linkage rows and the initial-available subset in the validation rows; say so and supply linked counts.
- **Contradicted-share percentages** quoted in text (2.44, 2.04, 2.75, 2.33, 4.07, 2.25, 5.07, 2.56, 0.75, 0.74, 0.76, 0.72) appear in no table; add a row.
- **ABE uniqueness** (line 426): one 1940 candidate per 1930 record does not yield one-to-one files; two 1930 records can share a sole candidate. State reciprocal uniqueness. Also "birth state matches exactly" should be the harmonized-birthplace rule for the foreign-born.
- **Table 9 conservative variant**: as written, exact matches are already unique within ±2 in both files, so the conservative restriction appears redundant although counts differ substantially; specify what "competing" means.
- **Token accuracy** (Table 1, A.3): whether padding positions count, how length errors score, pooled vs averaged.

## 4. Framing and positioning

### 4.1 Headline numbers [all three]

The 119% figure is a gain on a selected subset under one linker. For a user building a panel the relevant numbers are the full-sample gains, which differ sharply by linker (recomputed from Tables 5–8):

| Linker | Increase in all links | Increase in target links |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| ABE | +7.8% | +118% |
| Jaro-Winkler | +6.4% | +48% |
| HLINK | +2.5% | +14% |

The abstract should report the full-sample gain beside the target-set doubling, replace the +28% quality claim with the fixed-initial figure (≈+9%), and name the linker. The HLINK column shows that a linker which already tolerates spelling noise gains much less, which is itself a useful finding to state.

### 4.2 Contribution relative to the authors' own prior work [GPT, Gemini, Claude — check citations]

The claim that decomposing transcription into table identification, cell extraction and character recognition is "the key methodological innovation" (line 128) needs to be positioned against:

- Dahl, Johansen, Sørensen and Wittrock (2023), "HANA: A handwritten name database for offline handwritten text recognition," *Explorations in Economic History* — handwritten-name training data and transfer learning for Danish and U.S. census names.
- Dahl, Johansen, Sørensen, Westermann and Wittrock (2023), "Applications of machine learning in tabular document digitisation," *Historical Methods* — the layout-classification, segmentation and recognition pipeline.
- Petitpierre, Kramer and Rappo (2023), "An end-to-end pipeline for historical censuses processing," *IJDAR*.

Say what is inherited and what is new. The strongest distinctive contribution is the national-scale replacement experiment across several linkers, not the architecture. Also: LayoutParser is a general toolkit, not a newspaper-specific method (line 204); the comparison with "general OCR" is inadequate when handwriting recognizers such as TrOCR exist.

### 4.3 Representativeness vs. coverage [GPT, Gemini, Claude]

The evidence supports "more records for every group," not "narrower disparities" or "less biased estimates." The conclusion already says this (lines 785–787); the abstract and introduction should match it. Table 2 supports similarity on displayed observables only, not the broader claim that dropping 4.3M records "does not select a materially different population" (line 447).

### 4.4 Compute cost and dissemination [GPT, Claude]

Replace the 730,000 GPU-hour extrapolation with the realized cost of the 49-jurisdiction run (GPU model, training vs. inference, segmentation vs. recognition, throughput). State what researchers will receive: replacement names keyed to IPUMS identifiers, confidence scores, failure flags, code, weights, access terms. Qualify portability claims: the targeting step requires two existing transcriptions and census-specific templates.

### 4.5 Confidence scores [Gemini, GPT]

The introduction and Section 2.2 say confidence scores "prove valuable" for linking; they are never used in any linking exercise. Describe confidence-informed linkage as proposed, not demonstrated.

### 4.6 Missing citations [all three — check details]

## 8. Overall assessment and triage

**Strongest contribution.** A full-census demonstration that transcription is an adjustable input to historical linkage, with gains measured under three linkers and a mortality application. This is directly useful to anyone building historical panels.

**Biggest risk.** The paper currently attributes the gains to the machine-learning method without the FamilySearch comparison only the authors can run; its headline quality number changes the yardstick mid-comparison; and Tables 5–8 do not satisfy their own accounting identity. A referee who finds any one of these will doubt the rest.

**Three changes with the greatest payoff.**

1. Establish incremental value: FamilySearch baseline, four-cell decomposition, explicit replacement and fallback policy, and the public-availability framing.
2. Make the quality evidence auditable: reconcile Panels A and B, document the reference set, add fixed-initial and contradicted-among-verifiable rows, report gains and losses inside and outside the target set.
3. Align the text with the evidence: full-sample gains in the abstract, the fixed-initial quality figure, accurate description of Figure 3, removal of stale and forward-looking passages, and positioning against the authors' prior pipeline papers.
