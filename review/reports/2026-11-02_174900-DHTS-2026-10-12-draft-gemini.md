### 1. Summary

This paper develops a machine learning pipeline to re-transcribe handwritten first and last names from 1940 U.S. Census manuscript images and evaluates whether substituting those transcriptions for the standard IPUMS/Ancestry.com strings improves record linkage for the 28.2 percent of records where independent human transcriptions from Ancestry.com and FamilySearch.org disagree. Using a template-based point-set registration model (FilterReg) to segment table cells and an EfficientNetV2-S convolutional network with 40 position-specific character classification heads trained on roughly 392,000 first-name and 121,000 last-name concordant records, the authors generate new transcriptions for the 48 contiguous states and the District of Columbia. They report that replacing Ancestry names with their machine transcriptions on discordant 1940 records increases the 1930–1940 Abramitzky, Boustan, and Eriksson (ABE) exact-name linking rate on those discordant records by 119 percent (from 8.9 to 19.4 percent) and raises the overall linkage rate across all records by 8 percent (from 22.8 to 24.6 percent), while increasing the share of target-set links with matching middle initials by 28 percent (from 65.4 to 83.6 percent). The paper further reports that linking and middle-initial match gains hold across three linking algorithms (exact-name ABE, Jaro-Winkler ABE, and IPUMS HLINK), increase recall and reduce the share of links contradicted by an external set of FamilySearch genealogical tree links, carry over to linking 1940 census records to Social Security mortality records (BUNMD, where exact matches rise by 7.08 percent), and produce larger proportional gains in enumeration districts with low baseline legibility (20 percent in the bottom legibility quartile versus 1.6 percent in the top quartile).

### 2. Major Concerns

1. **Unidentified source of the linking gains and missing human-transcription baselines (Sections 2.1, 2.3, 3.2, and 4.1–4.2)**
* *Where*: Section 2.1 (p. 6), Section 2.3 (p. 9), Section 3.2 (p. 12), and Section 4.1–4.2 (pp. 15–21).
* *Why it matters*: The paper's empirical design requires observing *both* the Ancestry.com and FamilySearch.org human transcriptions in 1940 to define the 28.2 percent discordant "target set" where the replacement is performed; concordant records are left untouched (Section 3.2, p. 12; Section 4.1, p. 16). In Section 2.3 (p. 9), the authors report that on discordant records their ML transcription reproduces the FamilySearch string in 52 percent of cases and the Ancestry string in 14 percent of cases, matching neither in 24 percent of cases and failing to produce a usable transcription in 10 percent of cases. This raises two fundamental identification questions that the paper never tests:
1. **How much of the gain comes from the ML model versus simply substituting the already-available FamilySearch human transcription?** Because the target set can only be identified if the researcher already possesses the FamilySearch transcription alongside Ancestry's, the natural, zero-compute baseline is to substitute the FamilySearch transcription for Ancestry on discordant records (or to allow the linker to match on either human transcription). If 52 percent of ML replacements on discordant records are identical to FamilySearch, a large share—potentially the vast majority—of the reported linking gains may simply reflect cases where Ancestry made a transcription or formatting error and FamilySearch did not.
2. **How much of the gain comes from optical character recognition versus the unstated "ditto-resolution rule"?** On p. 9, the authors remark in passing that the 52 percent agreement with FamilySearch could arise "from the ditto-resolution rule our pipeline has in common with FamilySearch." Yet neither Section 2 nor Appendix A ever explains how the ML pipeline detects or resolves ditto marks (`"`) or horizontal dashes (`—`, visible in Figure 1, column 7) at inference time, given that all consecutive matching surnames were excluded from the last-name training set (Section 2.1, p. 6; Appendix A.4, p. 34). Nor can one tell from the PDF what string is used in the linking step for the 10 percent of discordant records (3.51 million records) where the ML model "returns no usable transcription at all" (p. 9)—whether those records revert to Ancestry, revert to FamilySearch, or become unlinkable.


* *What would resolve it*:
1. Add a baseline column in Tables 4–9 that substitutes the **FamilySearch** human transcription on the discordant target set (and a second baseline that takes the union of candidate matches across Ancestry and FamilySearch).
2. Decompose the linking and validation results on the discordant target set across the four mutually exclusive outcome cells reported on p. 9: (i) ML agrees with Ancestry (14%), (ii) ML agrees with FamilySearch (52%), (iii) ML outputs a novel name matching neither human transcription (24%), and (iv) ML fails to produce a usable transcription (10%). The genuine value-added of the vision model over existing human transcriptions resides primarily in cell (iii) and in its ability to adjudicate between (i) and (ii).
3. Explicitly document in Section 2.2 and Appendix A how ditto marks/dashes are detected and resolved at inference time, and how the 10 percent ML failure cases are handled in the linking pipeline.




2. **Negative spillovers (crowding out) onto concordant records and destruction of verified links (Section 4.1, Table 4; Section 4.2, Tables 5–8)**
* *Where*: Table 4 (p. 15) and Tables 5–8 (pp. 18–21).
* *Why it matters*: A closer inspection of the record counts across the target (discordant) tables and full-sample tables reveals that replacing transcriptions on discordant records destroys hundreds of thousands of existing links—including verified-correct links—among *concordant* records:
* In **Table 4 (p. 15)**, the linkage rate on the discordant `Target` sample ($N = 14,416,543$) rises from $0.089$ ($\approx 1,283,072$ links) to $0.194$ ($\approx 2,796,809$ links), a gross gain of **$\approx +1,513,737$ links**. Yet in the `All` sample ($N = 51,036,859$), the linkage rate rises from $0.228$ ($\approx 11,636,404$ links) to $0.246$ ($\approx 12,555,067$ links), a net gain of only **$\approx +918,663$ links**. Thus, **roughly 595,000 existing links (39 percent of the target-set gain) disappear among the 36.6 million concordant records** whose 1940 transcriptions were never touched.
* The exact same pattern appears in **Tables 5 and 6 (pp. 18–19)** (once the transposed `Old`/`New` columns in those tables are corrected; see Section 3 below). Under ABE, total links on discordant records (Table 6, $N = 15,003,537$) increase by $+1,520,423$ (from $1,284,239$ to $2,804,662$), and `Verified Correct` links increase by $+784,698$ (from $515,555$ to $1,300,253$). Yet in the full sample (Table 5, $N = 57,589,273$), total ABE links increase by only $+972,032$ (from $12,515,404$ to $13,487,436$), and `Verified Correct` links increase by only $+502,475$ (from $6,188,445$ to $6,690,920$). On the **42.6 million concordant records** (Table 5 minus Table 6), total links **fall by 548,391** and `Verified Correct` links **fall by 282,223** (with `415,501` concordant `Verified Correct` links removed in Table 5 Panel B compared to Table 6 Panel B: $563,429 - 147,928 = 415,501$). Similar losses on concordant records occur under Jaro-Winkler (a net loss of $272,930$ total links and $146,035$ `Verified Correct` links on concordant records) and HLINK (Tables 7 vs. 8: a net loss of $415,826$ total links and $181,547$ `Verified Correct` links on concordant records).
* From the PDF alone, it is impossible to tell whether these losses on concordant records in Tables 5–8 are entirely driven by **uniqueness collisions** (newly re-transcribed discordant 1940 records colliding with concordant 1940 records in the same birth state and $\pm 2$-year birth window, causing Step 2 of ABE to drop both), or whether the third codebase used in Section 4.2 replaced 1940 names on *all* records (including concordant ones, where the ML model has a 2.6 percent first-name and 5.5 percent last-name error rate in Table 1) rather than only on discordant records. Either way, even within the discordant sample itself (Table 6, Panel B), wholesale replacement removes $147,928$ `Verified Correct` ABE links ($226,946$ under HLINK in Table 8).


* *What would resolve it*:
1. Clarify whether Section 4.2 replaced transcriptions only on discordant records (as stated in Section 3.2 for Table 4) or across all records, and explicitly report the link changes on the **concordant** subsample alongside the discordant subsample.
2. Explain the uniqueness-collision mechanism and evaluate a linking rule that uses the ML transcription as a *supplement* (or uses the model's character-level confidence scores, emphasized in Section 2.2, to replace Ancestry strings only when ML confidence exceeds a threshold) rather than unconditionally overwriting human transcriptions and discarding half a million verified links.




3. **Confounded middle-initial validation in Table 4 and overstatement of the "+28% match quality" headline (Abstract, Section 1, Section 4.1)**
* *Where*: Abstract (p. 1), Section 1 (p. 2), and Section 4.1 / Table 4 (p. 15).
* *Why it matters*: The paper's headline quality claim is that machine transcriptions improve match quality on target records by **28 percent** (from 65.4 percent to 83.6 percent in Table 4). However, on p. 15 the text acknowledges that in Table 4, "Before" links are validated using Ancestry's 1940 middle initial whereas "After" links are validated using the ML model's own re-transcription of the 1940 middle initial: *"About half of this rise, 8 to 10 of its 18 percentage points, comes from the links themselves: it remains when every link, old and new, is scored against the same 1940 middle initial, taken from either the Ancestry or the FamilySearch transcription. The rest arises because each version is scored against its own transcription of the 1940 middle initial..."* And for the full sample (+1 percent in Table 4), p. 15 admits that *"most of which reflects the different reading of middle initials rather than different links."* Changing the validation yardstick at the same time as changing the links confounds link precision with middle-initial OCR repair. Indeed, in Table 6 (p. 19), where ABE links on discordant records are evaluated against a consistent middle-initial field, the middle-initial match rate increases from **76.81 percent to 83.51 percent** (+6.70 percentage points, or **+8.7 percent**—less than one-third of the headline +28 percent).
* *What would resolve it*: In Table 4 (and in the Abstract and Introduction), report middle-initial validation holding the 1940 middle-initial reference string fixed (showing both the Ancestry middle initial and the FamilySearch middle initial as separate rows). If the authors also wish to show that their model improves middle-initial transcriptions on the *same* links, report that separately so link quality and middle-initial transcription quality are not conflated.


4. **Independence and interpretation of the FamilySearch genealogical validation (Section 1, p. 3; Section 4.2, pp. 16–21)**
* *Where*: Section 1 (p. 3) and Section 4.2 (pp. 16–21, Tables 5–8).
* *Why it matters*: Three issues affect the external validation exercise in Section 4.2:
1. **Endogeneity of the FamilySearch truth set to FamilySearch census transcriptions**: The reference standard is "built from FamilySearch profiles" (p. 16). On FamilySearch.org, census records are attached to profiles largely through automated record hints generated from FamilySearch's own census transcriptions. When Ancestry's 1940 transcription contains an error on a discordant record while FamilySearch's transcription is accurate, that 1940 record is attached to the FamilySearch tree via its FamilySearch name. Because the ML model agrees with FamilySearch nearly four times as often as with Ancestry on discordant records (52% vs. 14%, p. 9), links generated from ML transcriptions will mechanically achieve much higher recall against a FamilySearch-indexed truth set than links generated from Ancestry transcriptions, even where Ancestry and FamilySearch are equally plausible on the manuscript page.
2. **Dilution of the "Directly Contradicted" share by Unverifiable links**: On pp. 3 and 16–17, the authors argue that because the absolute count of `Directly Contradicted` links rises under HLINK (from $501,010$ to $503,357$ in Table 7; from $116,233$ to $125,607$ in Table 8) and under discordant ABE (from $65,148$ to $71,863$ in Table 6), one should look instead at `Directly Contradicted` divided by *all* links (including `Unverifiable` links), which falls slightly in every specification (e.g., from $0.75\%$ to $0.74\%$ in Table 7 and from $0.76\%$ to $0.72\%$ in Table 8). However, roughly 50 to 70 percent of all links are `Unverifiable` (neither the 1930 nor the 1940 record appears in the FamilySearch truth set), and newly recovered links for harder-to-read records are even *more* likely to be absent from FamilySearch family trees (in Table 7 Panel B, $69.8\%$ of Added links are Unverifiable, raising the overall Unverifiable count by $+1,180,872$). Dividing `Directly Contradicted` by a denominator that includes tens of millions of `Unverifiable` links mechanically dilutes the contradicted rate whenever the unverifiable share rises.
3. **Higher error rates among Added verifiable links than among baseline links**: If one instead computes the contradicted share among **verifiable** links—$\text{Directly Contradicted} / (\text{Verified Correct} + \text{Directly Contradicted})$—then in Panel B of Tables 5, 7, and 8, the links **Added** by the new transcription have a substantially *higher* contradicted rate than the baseline `Old` links:
* Table 5 (Jaro-Winkler, full): **8.27%** on Added verifiable links ($75,388 / 911,261$) versus **4.73%** on Old verifiable links ($308,848 / 6,531,546$).
* Table 5 (ABE, full): **7.04%** on Added verifiable links ($80,670 / 1,145,385$) versus **5.27%** on Old verifiable links ($344,018 / 6,532,463$).
* Table 7 (HLINK, full): **8.01%** on Added verifiable links ($98,915 / 1,234,184$) versus **1.74%** on Old verifiable links ($501,010 / 28,858,051$).
* Table 8 (HLINK, discordant): **6.06%** on Added verifiable links ($58,897 / 971,727$) versus **1.87%** on Old verifiable links ($116,233 / 6,230,661$).




* *What would resolve it*: Report the contradicted share among *verifiable* links, $\text{Contradicted} / (\text{Verified} + \text{Contradicted})$, in Panel A and Panel B of Tables 5–8; discuss openly that marginal added links under HLINK and full-sample ABE/Jaro-Winkler have higher contradicted rates among verifiable pairs than baseline links (while still being verified correct 92–94% of the time); and acknowledge that FamilySearch tree links are not independent of FamilySearch census transcriptions.


5. **Mismatch between the text's claims about under-linked demographic groups and Figure 3 / Section 4.5 (Abstract, Section 1, Section 4.5)**
* *Where*: Abstract (p. 1), Section 1 (p. 2), and Section 4.5 / Figure 3 (pp. 23–25).
* *Why it matters*:
* In Section 1 (p. 2), the authors state that they build training data *"with particular attention to names from minority and immigrant populations."* Nothing in Section 2.1 or Appendix A.4 gives special weight or attention to minority or immigrant names; on the contrary, because training data are restricted to records where Ancestry and FamilySearch agree exactly, and foreign-born individuals have significantly higher disagreement rates (Table 3, p. 14), immigrant names are systematically *underrepresented* in the training set.
* In Section 4.5 (p. 23), the text directs the reader to Figure 3 by stating: *"since a marker sitting to the left and high up is a group that was poorly linked before and improved most."* Yet in the upper panel of Figure 3 (`% increase in linkage rate` against `Pre-improvement linkage rate`), **no group sits to the left and high up—the cloud slopes upward from bottom-left to top-right**. `Non-whites` have the lowest baseline linkage rate in the entire chart (11.0%) and also the **lowest proportional gain of any group (6.4%)**, followed by `No schooling` (11.9% baseline, 7.0% gain) and `Inmates` (15.0% baseline, 7.3% gain). Conversely, the highest proportional gains accrue to the most advantaged groups at the far right (`Some grad. sch.` at ~9.4%, `Professional` at ~9.2%, `Managers` at ~9.0%, and whites overall at 7.9%, from 24.1% to 26.0%). Thus, while absolute sample sizes rise for all groups, the transcription pipeline widens the linkage gap between white and non-white men both proportionally (+7.9% vs. +6.4%) and in percentage points (+1.90 pp vs. +0.70 pp).


* *What would resolve it*: Remove the claim in Section 1 (p. 2) that the training data were constructed with special attention to minority and immigrant names; revise the description of Figure 3 on p. 23 to accurately note that proportional linkage gains are positively correlated with baseline linkage rates across socio-demographic groups (whereas proportional gains in *middle-initial validation* in the lower panel are negatively correlated with baseline validation); and explain why non-white men see smaller gains (specifically, as shown in Table 3, Black men actually have a *lower* transcription disagreement rate than white men—8.4% vs. 9.1% of the discordant/congruent populations—so their low baseline linkage rate is driven more by name commonness, age misreporting, and migration than by handwriting disagreement).


6. **Flawed sample selection rule in the unexecuted 1% sample validation (Section 4.4, pp. 22–23)**
* *Where*: Section 4.4 (pp. 22–23).
* *Why it matters*: Aside from being unfinished ("Results from this validation analysis are forthcoming", p. 23), the proposed validation restricts the sample in Criterion (3) to records where *"at least one of our three transcriptions (Ancestry, FamilySearch census, or machine learning) matches the profile name exactly."* The next paragraph then hypothesizes that the ML model's errors are localized single-character substitutions (`n/r` or `a/o`), whereas human errors are multi-character differences. However, conditioning on at least one exact match (i) mechanically favors FamilySearch census transcriptions whenever the FamilySearch profile name was seeded from the 1940 FamilySearch census record, and (ii) drops every difficult record where all three transcriptions miss by at least one character—which is precisely the set needed to test whether the ML model has a smaller character edit distance (Levenshtein or Jaro-Winkler distance) to the profile name when no transcription is exact.
* *What would resolve it*: Either complete this analysis before publication—dropping Criterion (3) or reporting results both with and without it using character edit distance against profiles verified from non-1940-census vital records—or remove Section 4.4 from the paper.

4. **Inconsistent age cutoffs, denominators, and gender coverage across sections**
* **Age cutoff of 10+ vs. 8+**: Section 3.1 (p. 11) states: *"There were 55,347,491 males aged 10 or above in the 1940 census and thus potentially linkable to the 1930 census. We exclude children under 10 as they would not have appeared in the 1930 census."* Yet Section 4.2 (Tables 5–8, pp. 17–21) uses an age cutoff of **8 and above** in 1940 (`57,589,273` men in Table 5; `115,030,746` men and women in Table 7). Children aged 8 and 9 on April 1, 1940 were born between April 1930 and April 1932 and therefore did not exist at the time of the April 1, 1930 census. Including 2.24 million 8- and 9-year-old boys in Tables 5–6 (and ~4.5 million 8- and 9-year-old boys and girls in Tables 7–8) pads the denominators with unlinkable individuals and ensures that any link made for a true 8- or 9-year-old is a false positive caused by the $\pm 2$-year birth-year window.
* **All census records vs. records with all 4 name fields present**: In Table 4 (p. 15), the `All` denominator (`51,036,859`) restricts to men aged 10+ who have all four name fields and non-missing age in both human transcriptions, whereas in Table 5 (p. 18), the denominator (`57,589,273`) includes all men aged 8+ regardless of whether all four human name fields are present.

### 4. Framing and Positioning

1. **Headline numbers (Abstract and Section 1)**
* Leading with a **119 percent** linking rate increase and a **28 percent** match quality increase on the discordant subsample gives an exaggerated impression of what applied users will experience in practice. For economists constructing a linked 1930–1940 panel, the relevant metric is the full-population gain: **+8.0 percent** in Table 4 (+7.8% in Table 5 under ABE, +6.4% under Jaro-Winkler, +2.5% under HLINK in Table 7, and +7.1% in BUNMD in Table 9), alongside a **+0.3 to +0.9 percent** full-sample improvement in middle-initial agreement once the 1940 middle-initial yardstick is held fixed. The Abstract and Introduction should place the full-sample gains on equal footing with the target-set gains and state the unconfounded middle-initial improvement (+8.7% on discordant ABE records in Table 6, rather than the confounded +28% in Table 4).


2. **Positioning on character-level uncertainty and under-linked groups**
* Both the Introduction (pp. 2, 4) and Section 2.2 (pp. 7–8) emphasize that character-by-character prediction provides uncertainty measures for downstream linking decisions, and that the pipeline expands representation for minority and under-linked groups. Because uncertainty measures are never actually used in the linking exercises (as acknowledged in the Conclusion, p. 27) and proportional linking gains are slightly smaller for non-whites (+6.4%) than for whites (+7.9%), both claims should be tempered in the Abstract and Introduction to match the measured tone of the Conclusion.


3. **Related literature and missing citations**
* **Update working papers to published versions**: `Abramitzky et al. (2019a)` should be cited as its published version in the *Journal of Economic Literature* (2021, 59(3): 865–918).
* **Machine learning and historical census linkage**: Cite **Feigenbaum (2016)** ("Automated Census Record Linking: A Machine Learning Approach") on supervised ML census linking, and **Helgertz et al. (2022)** ("A new approach to historical census linkage: The IPUMS Multigenerational Longitudinal Panel," *Historical Methods*, 55(1): 12–29) for the methodology underlying HLINK/MLP (currently only the 2024 dataset release is cited). **Bleakley and Ferrie (2016)** (*QJE*) is also worth citing as an early benchmark using middle-initial match rates and transcription quality in linked census data.
* **Tabular handwriting recognition in economic history**: The segmentation and multi-head EfficientNet architecture in Appendix A clearly builds on the Danish historical transcription pipeline developed by two of the coauthors (**Dahl, Johansen, Sørensen, and Wittrock, 2022/2023**, on DARE / tabular HTR for historical vital and nurse records); citing that methodological work would clarify what is newly developed for the U.S. Census versus adapted from prior work.

### 7. Overall Assessment

* **Strongest contribution**: The paper demonstrates at full-census scale (more than 50 million records across 49 jurisdictions) that treating historical census transcriptions as improvable measurement objects rather than fixed inputs yields meaningful, durable gains in record linkage across multiple linking algorithms (exact ABE, Jaro-Winkler, HLINK) and external datasets (BUNMD mortality records), with the largest gains concentrated in the least legible enumeration districts where human transcribers fail most often.
* **Biggest risk at a journal**: A skeptical referee or editor will immediately notice two vulnerabilities: first, the pervasive table construction errors (transposed `Old`/`New` and `Congruent`/`Incongruent` entries across Tables 3, 5, 6, 7, and 8, misplaced numbers in Table 9, missing Panel A in Figure 2, and unfinished placeholder text in Section 4.4 and the Conclusion), which undermine confidence in the empirical execution; and second, the absence of a **FamilySearch human-transcription baseline**, leaving the reader unable to tell how much of the reported linking gain on discordant records comes from the machine learning model versus simply adopting FamilySearch's existing transcription (with which the model agrees 52 percent of the time) or losing concordant links to uniqueness collisions.
* **Three changes that would most improve the paper**:
1. **Benchmark against the FamilySearch human transcription and decompose the discordant target set**: Report linking and validation outcomes when substituting FamilySearch's transcription on discordant records, and break down the ML gains across the four cells on p. 9 (14% agrees with Ancestry, 52% agrees with FamilySearch, 24% novel ML transcription, 10% ML failure), while using model confidence scores (or a union/fallback rule) so that re-transcribing discordant records does not destroy ~400,000–550,000 existing links on concordant records.
2. **Audit and harmonize all tables, definitions, and samples**: Fix all transposed cells in Tables 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9; harmonize the sample universe across Tables 2–8 to men aged 10+ (removing unlinkable 8- and 9-year-olds who were not yet born in 1930); hold the 1940 middle-initial reference string fixed in Table 4; and report the contradicted share among *verifiable* links in Tables 5–8.
3. **Remove or complete unfinished material and align headline claims with the evidence**: Either execute the 1% sample transcription validation (without the restrictive exact-match filter in Criterion 3) or cut Section 4.4; remove stale passages claiming the 1940 census transcription is still in progress; and recalibrate the Abstract, Introduction, and Section 4.5 so that headline numbers reflect full-sample gains, unconfounded middle-initial validation, and the actual distribution of gains across demographic groups in Figure 3.
