## 1. Cell 1 framing, by-construction equality with Ancestry, and what Cell 1 actually contributes

Quoted text: "The most informative cell is the first, the 14.3 percent of the target set where our model's reading coincides with Ancestry's rather than FamilySearch's. There, Ancestry's names and ours link 21.6 and 21.4 percent of records, with 82.8 and 83.0 percent of links validated, while FamilySearch's 1940 names link only 13.0 percent, with 67.8 percent validated. ... On these records FamilySearch's reading is the less useful of the two for linkage, and our model, which reads the image without seeing either human transcription, arrives at the more useful one."

What is wrong or risky:

- By-construction equality: Because Cell 1 is defined by our model matching Ancestry's raw name string, Ours and Ancestry have the same 1940 names here by construction (the 0.2 pp difference — 21.4% vs 21.6%, about 4,123 links — arises only because changing names in Cells 2–4 creates a few new uniqueness collisions in 1940). Presenting Ancestry (21.6%) and Ours (21.4%) side by side without noting this makes them sound like independent outcomes.
- Contribution relative to Ancestry vs. FamilySearch: Relative to the paper's Ancestry baseline, Cell 1 contributes zero net linkage gain (−0.2 pp × 14.3% = −0.03 pp on the target set). Rather, Cell 1's role is loss avoidance: a naive replacement of Ancestry with FamilySearch 1940 would destroy 8.6 pp of links in this cell ((21.6−13.0)% × 2.06M ≈ 177,000 links), and the image model avoids that loss by siding with Ancestry when Ancestry is right.
- Unexploited defense against a 1930-baseline objection: A sceptical referee will ask whether Ancestry/Ours links better than FamilySearch 1940 in Cell 1 simply because the 1930 census is also transcribed by Ancestry (shared house-style spelling conventions). Table 11 (1930 & 1940 vs. 1930 columns) directly refutes this: when 1930 is switched to FamilySearch names, 1940 Ancestry still beats 1940 FamilySearch by the same 8.6 pp margin (22.8% vs. 14.2%, compared with 21.6% vs. 13.0%). Cite those two numbers.

Recommendation: Keep the core claim (supported as a statement about the model discriminating between conflicting human readings), drop the subjective label "The most informative cell", state explicitly that Ours equals Ancestry here by definition, and cite the 1930 vs. 1930 & 1940 columns.

Proposed replacement wording: "In the first cell—the 14.3 percent of the target set where our model's reading coincides with Ancestry's rather than FamilySearch's—our names are identical to Ancestry's by construction (their linkage rates of 21.4 and 21.6 percent differ slightly only because name changes in other cells affect uniqueness in 1940). On these records, FamilySearch's 1940 names link only 13.0 percent (and 14.2 percent against 22.8 percent when FamilySearch's names are also used in 1930). Thus, where our image-only model sides with Ancestry over FamilySearch, Ancestry's reading is indeed the more useful one for linkage, and our model avoids the loss of roughly 173,000 links that wholesale substitution of FamilySearch's 1940 names would entail."

## 2. Cell 1 validation gap and asymmetric middle-initial contamination across Cells 1 and 2

Quoted text: "The gap persists when every version is scored against Ancestry's middle initials, at 83.0 percent for our links against 77.5 percent for FamilySearch's."

What is wrong or risky:

- Overstatement of persistence: In Cell 1, switching from own initials to Ancestry's initials shrinks the validation gap between Ours and FamilySearch 1940 by nearly two-thirds, from 15.2 pp (83.0−67.8) to 5.5 pp (83.0−77.5; and to 4.7 pp, 83.0−78.3, for 1930 & 1940). Saying merely "The gap persists" without noting that two-thirds of the own-initial gap vanishes will strike a referee as selective framing.
- Why the two validation measures move in opposite directions between Cell 1 and Cell 2: In Cell 1 (selected on FamilySearch's 1940 name being wrong and Ancestry's being right), FamilySearch's validation rate jumps by +9.7 pp (67.8% → 77.5%) when scored with Ancestry's initials. In Cell 2 (selected on Ancestry's 1940 name being wrong and FamilySearch's being right), both Ours and FamilySearch 1940 drop by 11.4 pp (87.5%/87.6% → 76.1%/76.2%) when scored with Ancestry's initials — a drop the prose never mentions. Why? When a human transcriber misreads a 1940 line or mis-splits a compound first name into given name + middle initial, that same transcription's 1940 middle initial is also corrupted. In Cell 1 FamilySearch's 1940 transcription is the broken one, so scoring FamilySearch links with FamilySearch's own initials depresses its rate (67.8%) while Ancestry's initials are clean (77.5%). Conversely, in Cell 2 (51.5% of the target set) Ancestry's 1940 transcription is the broken one; scoring Ours and FamilySearch 1940 against Ancestry's corrupted initials penalizes both by 11.4 pp (and by ~8–9 pp overall in Table 9: 83.6 → 75.7 for Ours, 83.4 → 74.8 for FS 1940).

Recommendation: Acknowledge explicitly that the Cell 1 validation gap narrows from 15.2 to 5.5 pp under Ancestry initials, and briefly explain why corrupted 1940 name transcriptions also corrupt that transcription's middle initials in both Cell 1 and Cell 2.

Proposed replacement wording: "Much of the validation gap in the first cell reflects errors in FamilySearch's 1940 middle initials on records where its name transcription went awry: when every version is instead scored against Ancestry's middle initials, FamilySearch's validation rate rises from 67.8 to 77.5 percent, narrowing the gap with our links (83.0 percent) from 15.2 to 5.5 percentage points. (The converse holds in the second cell, where Ancestry's 1940 transcription is the faulty one: scoring against Ancestry's middle initials lowers the share validated for both our links and FamilySearch's from 87.5 and 87.6 percent to 76.1 and 76.2 percent.)"

## 3. Cell 2 linkage gain decomposition and baseline ambiguity

Quoted text: "In the second cell, half of the target set, our names coincide with FamilySearch's, and the two give nearly identical results: 25.9 and 25.7 percent linked, with 87.5 and 87.6 percent validated, against 6.7 and 53.0 percent for Ancestry's names. This cell accounts for nearly all of our gain in linkage on the target set."

The arithmetic is right relative to Ancestry: Cell 2 provides (25.9−6.7)% × 7,428,567 = 1,426,285 additional links (+9.89 pp on the target set), 94.2% of the total target-set gain of (19.4−8.9)% × 14,416,543 = 1,513,737 links (+10.5 pp), with the remaining ~6% from Cell 3 (+76,231, +0.53 pp) and Cell 4 (+18,836, +0.13 pp), offset by Cell 1 (−4,123, −0.03 pp). However, because the subsection shifts between two benchmarks (Ancestry and FamilySearch 1940), a reader may wonder how Ours achieves 94% of FamilySearch 1940's overall gain (10.5 vs 11.2 pp) if Ours only matches FamilySearch on 51.5% of the target set. The reason: in Cell 1 staying with Ancestry beats FamilySearch 1940 by +173,152 links (+1.20 pp), which almost exactly cancels Ours trailing FamilySearch 1940 in Cell 3 by −180,182 links (−1.25 pp).

Recommendation: Keep the claim, specify that the gain is over Ancestry, and give the exact percentage.

Proposed replacement wording: "This cell accounts for 94 percent of our total gain in linkage over Ancestry on the target set (1.43 million of the 1.51 million additional links)."

## 4. Cell 3 excuse blaming 1930 transcription errors (contradicted by Table 11)

Quoted text: "... A failure to link does not show that our reading is wrong, since the 1930 record may carry an error of its own, but where our transcription departs from both human readings it adds few links."

- The 1930-error excuse is contradicted by your own table: In Cell 3, FamilySearch 1940 links 14.1% of these exact same 1940 records to the exact same 1930 Ancestry records (76.1% own-initial / 68.4% Ancestry-initial validation), compared with only 8.9% for Ours. Moreover, replacing 1930 Ancestry names with 1930 FamilySearch names barely moves Cell 3 linkage (6.7% → 6.8%), and replacing both raises it only from 14.1% to 15.6%. Thus, for the 5.2% of Cell 3 (~180,000 records) that FamilySearch 1940 links and our model does not, the 1930 Ancestry record does not carry a link-blocking error. A referee will flag this as special pleading.
- Missing Ancestry-initial validation comparison in Cell 3: Under own initials Ours trails FamilySearch 1940 by 12.2 pp (63.9 vs 76.1); under Ancestry initials by only 3.6 pp (64.8 vs 68.4). Mentioning the Ancestry-initial numbers makes Cell 3 look less poor on link quality.

Recommendation: Drop the clause "A failure to link does not show that our reading is wrong, since the 1930 record may carry an error of its own," and state straightforwardly what Cell 3 shows (including Ancestry-initial validation).

Proposed replacement wording: "Here our names raise the linkage rate only from 6.7 to 8.9 percent and the share validated from 59.8 to 63.9 percent (64.8 percent with Ancestry's initials), whereas FamilySearch's 1940 names link 14.1 percent with 76.1 percent validated (68.4 percent with Ancestry's initials). Thus, on the quarter of the target set where our transcription departs from both human readings, it adds about 76,000 links over Ancestry—5 percent of our total target-set gain—but falls well short of FamilySearch's 1940 transcription."

## 5. Cell 4 description ("fills from Ancestry's transcription") and omission of FamilySearch's 15.0% linkage rate

Quoted text: "In the fourth cell the model produced no output for at least one name field, which our version fills from Ancestry's transcription, and its linkage rate stays close to Ancestry's, at 8.4 against 6.9 percent."

- Why Ours rises from 6.9% to 8.4% (and validation from 72.8% to 80.1%): the wording makes it sound as if Cell 4 records simply revert to Ancestry. In fact, when the model produces no output for only one field, the other field still uses the model's transcription. That partial substitution is why linkage rises 1.5 pp (+22% relative, ~18,800 links) and validation rises 72.8 → 80.1 (74.5 with Ancestry initials).
- Omission of FamilySearch 1940 in Cell 4: the text reports FamilySearch 1940's rate for Cells 1–3 but omits that it links 15.0% (83.8% validated) in Cell 4. That 6.6 pp shortfall on 8.7% of the target set ((15.0−8.4)% × 1,255,726 = 82,878 links, −0.57 pp) accounts for more than 80% of the entire net gap between Ours (19.4%) and FamilySearch 1940 (20.1%). (Cell 1's +1.20 pp and Cell 2's +0.10 pp nearly cancel Cell 3's −1.25 pp, leaving Cell 4's −0.57 pp and the unclassified residual's −0.18 pp to explain the −0.7 pp overall gap.) Pointing out that the small overall shortfall is largely driven by the 8.7% of records where the model abstained on a field is both transparent and informative.

Proposed replacement wording: "In the fourth cell (8.7 percent of the target set), the model produced no output for one or both name fields; our version fills the missing field from Ancestry's transcription while retaining the model's reading where available, raising the linkage rate modestly from 6.9 to 8.4 percent, compared with 15.0 percent for FamilySearch's 1940 names."

## 6. The closing summary sentence

Quoted text: "In short, our transcriptions do as well as FamilySearch's on the records where they agree with it, better on the records where they agree with Ancestry, and worse on the records where they agree with neither."

- Tautological phrasing on Cells 1 and 2: where our transcriptions agree with FamilySearch they are FamilySearch's; where they agree with Ancestry they are Ancestry's. Saying ours do "better" in Cell 1 sounds as if the model improved on the human transcriptions, when in reality Ancestry outperforms FamilySearch there and our model correctly selects Ancestry's reading.
- Omission of Cell 4 (8.7%).
- Missed takeaway: the model's advantage over FamilySearch in Cell 1 (+173,000) nearly offsets its disadvantage in Cell 3 (−180,000), so the small net gap (19.4 vs 20.1) is mainly due to records where the model produced no output (Cell 4).

Proposed replacement wording: "In short, when our model agrees with one of the two human transcriptions—FamilySearch on half the target set, Ancestry on a seventh—the transcription it selects is the more effective one for linkage; where it departs from both (a quarter of the target set) or abstains on a field (a tenth), it adds few links over Ancestry and trails FamilySearch, with the gain over FamilySearch in the first cell nearly offsetting the shortfall in the third."

## 7. Target set vs. All records in Table 9 (unmentioned 1.0 pp gap on All records)

On the Target set the 94% arithmetic holds. But the All rows, which the text never discusses, show: Ancestry 22.8%, Ours 24.6% (+1.8 pp, ≈919,000 links), FamilySearch 1940 25.6% (+2.8 pp, ≈1,429,000 links). Across All records Ours delivers only 1.8/2.8 = 64.3% (not 94%) of FamilySearch 1940's gain and trails it by 1.0 pp (≈510,000 links), despite trailing by only 0.7 pp (≈101,000) on the Target set.

Backing out the implied rate on the 36,620,316 non-target (concordant) records: Ancestry ≈ 28.27%; FamilySearch 1940 ≈ 27.76% (the −0.51 pp drop is purely new 1940 uniqueness collisions created by fixing target-set names, since FS 1940 = Ancestry on the non-target set); Ours ≈ 26.65%. That is, on the 36.6M concordant records FamilySearch 1940 leaves the agreed human transcription untouched, whereas Ours replaces the agreed transcription with the model's prediction, losing an additional 1.11 pp (~409,000 links) on concordant records (and lowering All own-initial validation from 87.9% for FS 1940 to 87.1% for Ours).

## 8. Interpretive risk: non-random selection across cells in Table 11

Ancestry linkage across cells: 21.6, 6.7, 6.7, 6.9; FamilySearch 1930 & 1940: 14.2, 28.7, 15.6, 16.4. Cells 1 and 2 select on records where one human transcription was clear enough for the image model to reproduce exactly; Cells 3 and 4 select on degraded handwriting or unusual names where even FS in both censuses reaches only 15.6% and 16.4%. Levels should be compared across columns within a cell, not across cells.

Proposed wording (end of paragraph 3): "Because the partition reflects both which human transcription erred and the legibility of the underlying image—even with FamilySearch names in both censuses, linkage is 28.7 percent in the second cell against 15.6 and 16.4 percent in the third and fourth—the meaningful comparisons in Table \ref{tab:fs_cells} are across columns within each cell."
