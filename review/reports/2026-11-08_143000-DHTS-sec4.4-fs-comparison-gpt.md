The subsection supports a useful comparison of linkage procedures, and the two "about 94%" calculations are correct. Its main weakness is the distinction between net linkage gains, links unavailable from either human transcription, and transcription accuracy. The tables establish the first; they do not yet establish the second or directly measure the third.

I recomputed the quantities below from the tabulars. Counts reported directly in the tables are exact; link counts reconstructed from the one-decimal linkage rates are necessarily approximate.

## 1. The tables do not answer whether the "neither" cell produces links unavailable from the two human transcriptions. Drop that inference unless you supply link-set overlaps.

"That 24% is the part a second human transcription could not give you." / "…where our transcription departs from both human readings it adds few links."

Table 11 establishes that, in cell 3, the model produces a net increase relative to Ancestry of approximately 3,465,034 × (0.089 − 0.067) = 76,231 links. It does not establish how many model links are unavailable using FamilySearch, or unavailable using either human transcription.

For example, the approximately 308,388 model-linked records in this cell could all already be among the approximately 488,570 records linked using FamilySearch's 1940 names. Alternatively, many could be different records. Both possibilities are compatible with the reported marginal rates. Among records linked by both procedures, the destination in 1930 could also differ.

A new raw name is not necessarily a new linkage key. Raw strings can differ but become identical after cleaning. If cell membership compares the complete first–last-name pair, cell 3 may also include a model first name matching one human source and a last name matching the other. Such a combination is absent from either human's complete name but does not necessarily contain a newly recovered name field.

Drop the handoff's inference that the entire 24% represents information that a second human transcription could not provide. The subsection's literal description of raw disagreement can remain.

To answer the referee's incremental-link question directly, generate overlap counts from the existing link files: model links absent from the union of the Ancestry and FamilySearch-1940 link sets, distinguishing newly linked 1940 people from different 1930 destinations for people already linked. Neither a 1930 model application nor a Section 2 revision is needed.

Suggested replacement for the concluding claim about cell 3: "Within this cell, our procedure increases the number linked by approximately 76,000 relative to Ancestry. The marginal linkage rates do not establish how many model links are unavailable using either human transcription."

## 2. "Percent of their links are validated" gives the wrong apparent denominator and overstates what initial agreement establishes.

"…83.4 percent of their links are validated, against 65.4 and 83.6 percent."

The denominator is links with both scoring initials observed, not all links. For FamilySearch's 1940 substitution, Table 9 implies approximately 14,416,543 × 0.201 = 2,897,725 target-set links, but only 651,573 enter the own-initial calculation: approximately 22.5% of links. For Ours, approximately 2,796,809 records link and 618,732 are scored: approximately 22.1%.

Middle-initial agreement is a proxy for link quality, not confirmation of identity. The eligible links have not been shown to represent the remaining roughly three-quarters of links.

The term "share validated" can remain as the paper's defined label, but the prose should state the actual event and denominator at first use. The same problem recurs in "with 82.8 and 83.0 percent of links validated."

Suggested replacement: "Among links with middle initials observed in both censuses, the initials agree in 83.4 percent of FamilySearch-1940 links, compared with 65.4 percent of Ancestry links and 83.6 percent of our links." Add: "These agreement rates are a link-quality diagnostic for the subset with observed initials."

## 3. The Ancestry-initials check is useful, but it fixes the transcription source—not the linked pairs, validation sample, or truth standard.

"…so that every version is checked against the same initials…"

Keep this check, but describe precisely what it holds fixed. Different procedures link different people and can select different 1930 destinations. Consequently, even when initials always come from Ancestry, the linked pairs and the subset with two observed initials differ.

The scoring choice materially changes the apparent gaps:

| Comparison from Table 11 | Own-initial gap | Ancestry-initial gap |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cell 1: Ours minus FamilySearch 1940 | 83.0 − 67.8 = 15.2 pp | 83.0 − 77.5 = 5.5 pp |
| Cell 3: FamilySearch 1940 minus Ours | 76.1 − 63.9 = 12.2 pp | 68.4 − 64.8 = 3.6 pp |

Is the Ancestry-initials comparison fair to FamilySearch in cell 1? It is a legitimate common-source sensitivity check, but not an independent, error-free adjudication. Ancestry's initial errors may be associated with its other name errors and with the agreement criterion defining cell 1. The tables do not identify the direction or magnitude of any resulting bias. Switching to Ancestry initials raises FamilySearch's cell-1 score from 67.8% to 77.5%, so it would be wrong to presume this scoring choice mechanically penalizes FamilySearch.

Is FamilySearch-derived validation circular? Not merely because names and initials come from the same provider; initials do not enter matching. The supported concern is correlated transcription error and changing availability. If the raw first-name comparison used to define cells includes the middle initial, that should be disclosed: some agreement between validation readings would then be built into cell membership.

Suggested replacement: "Using Ancestry's middle initials in both years fixes the transcription source used for this check, although the linked pairs and the subset with both initials observed can still differ across versions."

## 4. The narrow cell 1 claim is supported; the language should describe conditional linkage performance rather than independent adjudication of the reading.

"The most informative cell is the first…" / "On these records FamilySearch's reading is the less useful of the two for linkage, and our model, which reads the image without seeing either human transcription, arrives at the more useful one."

The descriptive ranking is supported: within cell 1, Ours links 21.4% vs 13.0% for FamilySearch 1940, and initial agreement is higher under both scoring schemes. Ancestry itself links 21.6%, confirming that the informative comparison is the Ancestry/model side against FamilySearch.

But agreement with Ancestry defines the cell. This is not an independent test showing that the model generally selects the correct human reading. Inserting the inference-input statement here can suggest a stronger form of validation than this selected comparison supplies. The benchmark is also specific: Ancestry remains the 1930 reference transcription, and linkage uses this exact-name algorithm. A reading can be more useful for matching that reference without being more faithful to the 1940 image.

Selection into cells does not invalidate the within-cell comparison of procedures on the same 1940 records. It prevents treating differences across cells as causal effects.

Drop "the most informative cell." Cell 3 is central to the referee's question about incremental value.

Suggested replacement: "Within this cell, defined by our model agreeing with Ancestry, our linkage procedure yields more links and higher middle-initial agreement than substituting FamilySearch's names in 1940, with Ancestry retained in 1930."

## 5. Explain why identical raw names produce different linkage rates. This is not a rounding issue.

Cell 1: Ancestry 21.6%, Ours 21.4%. Cell 2: Ours 25.9%, FamilySearch 1940 25.7%.

A reader will ask why equal names, the same other linking fields, and the same 1930 reference do not give equal results. Plausible explanation: rerunning linkage on the full file changes competing records and uniqueness, so a focal record's outcome can change even when its own name does not. Other possibilities involve differences between the raw classification strings and the actual processed linking fields. Verify and state the mechanism; do not assume it.

The notes must also distinguish stratifying the full linkage runs by cell from rerunning linkage separately within each cell. If Table 11 comes from separate within-cell runs, its results cannot be treated as an additive decomposition of Table 9.

Suggested note, if confirmed: "The cell results stratify the full-sample linkage runs by the 1940 record's raw-name agreement classification. Changes to other records can affect uniqueness, so a record's linkage outcome can differ across versions even when its own name is unchanged."

## 6. "Most of the gain" is supported as a target-set net-yield comparison; "at the same share validated" should be weakened.

(19.4 − 8.9)/(20.1 − 8.9) = 10.5/11.2 = 0.9375. About 94% is correct. This is the ratio of net increases in numbers linked relative to Ancestry — not the percentage of FamilySearch's particular links recovered, nor the percentage of correct links recovered. The model still produces approximately 101,000 fewer target-set links than FamilySearch 1940.

"At the same share validated" is unnecessarily strong: own-initial 83.6 vs 83.4; Ancestry-initial 75.7 vs 74.8. "Similar" is reasonable; equivalence of accuracy has not been established.

Suggested replacement: "Relative to Ancestry, our procedure achieves approximately 94 percent of the net increase in the target-set linkage rate obtained by substituting FamilySearch's names in 1940, with similar middle-initial agreement among links that can be scored."

Keep the target-set restriction explicit. For the All row the ratio is (24.6 − 22.8)/(25.6 − 22.8) = 1.8/2.8 ≈ 64.3%. An unqualified conclusion that the model captures nearly all the second transcription's linkage benefit would not be supported.

## 7. The Target and All rows imply substantial effects outside the target, if the samples are nested.

ΔL_Target ≈ 14,416,543 × (0.194 − 0.089) = 1,513,737; ΔL_All ≈ 51,036,859 × (0.246 − 0.228) = 918,663. The complement therefore loses approximately 595,074 links; the implied non-target linkage rate falls from about 28.27% to 26.65%. Far too large for rounding.

Not automatically an error: full-file uniqueness effects could account for it, or the procedure may change names or processing outside the target. But it is important for interpreting the overall benefit. Explain what happens outside the target and why these links decline.

## 8. Cell 2's "nearly all" statement is arithmetically correct, provided Table 11 stratifies the aggregate linkage runs. Specify net gain.

7,428,567 × (0.259 − 0.067) / [14,416,543 × (0.194 − 0.089)] = 1,426,285 / 1,513,737 ≈ 94.2%. Keep the claim, but make its meaning explicit: an accounting decomposition by the originating 1940 record's cell; it does not measure gross additions, identify which Ancestry links were retained, or establish causation.

Suggested replacement: "Records in this cell account for approximately 94 percent of the net increase in the number linked on the target set."

## 9. The cell 3 paragraph is directionally honest, but "only" and "few" need an explicit scale. Include the common-source scoring result.

The increase is 2.2 pp, ≈32.8% relative to the Ancestry rate, ≈76,000 net links, ≈5.0% of the overall target-set net increase. "Few" is defensible relative to the overall gain; less so as an unqualified description of the absolute number or proportional improvement. Under Ancestry initials the scores are 59.8 (Ancestry), 64.8 (Ours), 68.4 (FS 1940): the ordering survives while the model–FamilySearch gap is appreciably smaller than under own initials.

Suggested replacement: "The increase over Ancestry is 2.2 percentage points, or approximately 76,000 net links, accounting for about 5 percent of the overall target-set increase. The resulting linkage rate remains below FamilySearch's 14.1 percent. Middle-initial agreement is also lower than FamilySearch's under either scoring scheme."

Keep the statement that failure to link does not establish that the model's reading is wrong. That is an appropriate limitation, not an excuse requiring removal.

## 10. Drop the causal explanation for the both-years advantage.

"…a gain our transcriptions cannot yet match because we have not applied our model to the 1930 census."

You do not know that the absence of a 1930 model application explains the gap, or that applying the model there would close it. Effects interact: FS in 1930 raises the target-set rate by 0.1 pp when 1940 uses Ancestry but by 2.2 pp when 1940 already uses FamilySearch. FS 1940 also exceeds Ours by 0.7 pp before any 1930 substitution.

Suggested replacement: "Using FamilySearch's names in both censuses raises the target-set linkage rate to 22.3 percent. We do not have the analogous model-in-both-years comparison because our model has been applied only to 1940."

## 11. Cell 4 should report the FamilySearch benchmark and explain partial fallback. Its validation issue is changing coverage, not simply a small denominator.

6.9% → 8.4% is 1.5 pp, ≈21.7% relative; "stays close" is subjective. FS 1940 links 15.0% in this cell, which the paragraph omits. Missing output for one field does not imply the whole record retains Ancestry's name. Ours produces ≈105,481 links in this cell; its own-initial denominator of 9,531 covers ≈9.0%, the Ancestry-initial denominator of 26,489 ≈25.1%. The concern is which links are scored, not denominator size.

Suggested replacement: "In the incomplete-output cell, available model fields are retained and missing fields are supplied by Ancestry. This version links 8.4 percent of records, compared with 6.9 percent under Ancestry and 15.0 percent under FamilySearch's 1940 substitution."

## 12. The closing summary has the right ordering for the first three cells, but must specify FamilySearch 1940 and acknowledge the incomplete-output cell.

Broadly fair relative to FS 1940-only substitution; not a summary against every FamilySearch column (cell 2: Ours 25.9% vs FS both years 28.7%). It omits cell 4 (8.7%), where the model-based procedure links substantially fewer records than FS 1940. "Better" and "worse" should refer to reported linkage outcomes, not transcription fidelity.

Suggested replacement: "Relative to substituting FamilySearch's names in 1940 alone, our procedure yields similar linkage rates and initial agreement in the FamilySearch-agreement cell, higher rates in the Ancestry-agreement cell, and lower rates in the neither-agreement cell. It also links fewer records in the incomplete-output cell."

## 13. The residual groups require a local reconciliation of the sample and classification definitions.

If Target is a subset of the complete-field All sample under the same missingness definition, how can 741 target records have an empty human field? If target membership means disagreement between the human names, why does the target include records whose raw human strings agree? These may be explainable by different source versions, processing rules, or classification joins; the notes do not identify the explanation. Specify the exact target definition used in the linkage files and its relationship to the raw-string classifier. If there is an implementation error, regenerate the tables.

The four displayed cells cover 14,210,659 records (98.5719%); residuals 205,884 (1.4281%). Calling Table 10 the "same partition" is acceptable only with those residual categories explicitly acknowledged.

## 14. "Own transcription of the middle initials" is not sufficiently defined for the hybrid versions.

The notes should state: which source supplies the scoring initials in each year for every version (Ours cannot have a model-generated 1930 initial); whether initials are extracted from the selected first-name string or supplied separately; what happens when the model or FamilySearch supplies a first name but no middle initial; whether initial fallback follows the same rule as name fallback; that each percentage uses that version's linked pairs with both scoring initials observed.

Suggested general note: "Each initial-agreement percentage is calculated among that version's linked pairs for which both scoring initials are observed. The first scoring block uses the source-specific initials defined below; the second always uses Ancestry's initials in both years."
