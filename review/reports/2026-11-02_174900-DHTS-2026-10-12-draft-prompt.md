You are acting as an expert referee for an economics journal. Attached is a draft paper, "Improving Historical Census Transcriptions: A Machine Learning Approach." Please read the entire paper, including all tables, table notes, figures, and the appendix, before writing anything.

Context: the paper trains a machine learning model to re-transcribe handwritten names from 1940 U.S. census images, substitutes those transcriptions for the commercial (Ancestry.com) ones on records where two independent human transcriptions disagree, and reports the effect on linking 1930 to 1940 census records and 1940 census records to Social Security death records. The intended audience is economists and economic historians who build linked historical microdata.

Write a full referee report with the following structure:

1. Summary (one paragraph): what the paper does and what it claims, in your words.

2. Major concerns. These are issues that bear on whether the main claims are supported: identification of what the gains are attributable to, the validity and independence of the validation evidence, sample definitions and comparability across tables, missing comparisons or baselines a skeptical reader would demand, and any place where the text's interpretation goes beyond what the tables show. For each, say specifically which section, table, or passage it concerns, why it matters, and what analysis or change would resolve it.

3. Internal consistency. Check the numbers quoted in the text against the tables, check that definitions (samples, denominators, age cutoffs, the meaning of "validated," "verified," "contradicted," "recall") are consistent across sections, and flag any passage that appears stale relative to the rest of the paper (e.g., text describing work as planned or in progress when other sections report it as done).

4. Framing and positioning. Is the contribution stated accurately? Are the headline numbers the right ones to lead with? Is the related literature complete, and are there specific papers that should be cited?

5. Structure and exposition. Sections that are redundant, too long, too thin, or in the wrong place; passages a reader will find unclear.

6. Minor and editorial. Typos, notation, table formatting, caption and note issues.

7. Overall assessment: what you see as the paper's strongest contribution, what you see as the biggest risk at a journal, and the three changes that would most improve it.

Be specific and concrete throughout: cite page or section numbers and quote short phrases where helpful. Do not pad; a concern you cannot tie to a specific passage or table is not worth including. Do not summarize the paper beyond item 1. Do not propose rewrites of full paragraphs; describe what should change and why. If a claim seems wrong, explain the reasoning rather than just asserting it. If something cannot be assessed from the PDF alone (for instance because it depends on code or data you cannot see), say so explicitly rather than guessing.
